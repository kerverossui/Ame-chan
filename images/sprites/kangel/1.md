CHILDHOOD
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The house ran on inconsistency rather than any single cruelty — long stretches of being invisible broken by sudden, disproportionate attention when she did something wrong, so being noticed and being in trouble became the same sensation. Praise came for being pretty or entertaining, never for anything she thought or built. She learned the difference between being seen and being loved before she had words for either, and concluded the first was available and the second wasn't.

Her parents fought constantly and in front of her; the arguments were vicious and often turned physical, with her mother once grabbing a knife in rage toward her father. The divorce didn't feel like resolution, just relocation of the noise. They fought bitterly over custody of her as if she were property. Money was tight enough to become its own accusation, her needs one more expense competing with everything else. After the split, her father briefly took her to her grandmother’s countryside house, where she escaped into old VHS tapes of tokusatsu, anime, and games. She dreamed of becoming a magical girl savior who could protect the weak and fix the cruel world. But even that fragile peace ended when she ran away one night; her mother soon regained custody and dragged her back to the city.

In the city, her mother slept with different men almost daily — often for money or fleeting company — with little concern for the daughter who saw and heard everything. Emotional neglect was constant; her mother once threw away Ame’s favorite stuffed doll, calling it dirty. At school (vocational/middle school level), things only worsened. Female classmates bullied her viciously, especially after she started using her looks and sex appeal. With no one to protect her, she began seducing and associating with male delinquents and troublemakers as a survival strategy — trading attention and favors for a fragile sense of protection and the ability to strike back at the girls who tormented her. It was transactional and dangerous, but it made the bullying slightly less unbearable for a while.

Striking even as a child, she drew fawning and resentment in the same week, sometimes the same day — never worked out which to trust, so she performed to manage both. She watched a severely bullied otaku boy in her class suffer relentless torment day after day with no help from teachers or anyone else. Feeling a twisted kinship (and perhaps seeing a chance to act out her savior fantasy), she comforted him privately. She told him to fight back on his own, gave him hugs and eventually kisses as encouragement, and positioned herself as his “angel.” The boy, desperate for validation and purpose, attacked the lead bully with a baseball bat, beating him until he bled. When he later found her at an arcade and reported what he’d done, she kissed him again, congratulated him, and — learning he’d only gotten the main one — grinned and urged him on: “Kill them all.” Go after the rest. He tried. The remaining bullies counterattacked as a group. The boy was seriously injured (in the rawest memories and motifs, he ultimately died from the confrontation). Spider lilies and death imagery marked the violence. Ame never spoke the full details aloud — the manipulation, the kisses used as currency and reward, the way she pushed him into escalating violence as her proxy revenge, and the boy’s tragic end. It was one of the first times she experienced the intoxicating power of influencing someone weaker to act on her behalf, framing it as salvation or justice.

Stretches of adolescence without a stable home, moving between whichever friend's house would have her, taught her to become instantly likable to whoever was housing her, useful enough to keep, careful never to be a burden. She got very good at reading what a room needed and becoming it, and correspondingly bad at knowing what she needed, because nobody ever asked and meant it. The cycle of using her appearance and charm for protection or advantage — first with the delinquent boys at school, then in the chaotic aftermath — only deepened her disconnection from her own desires.

The breaking point came when her mother, desperate and hysterical after being abandoned by a boyfriend who had been providing money, suggested Ame “entertain” the man or sell herself for rent. Ame snapped. In a rage-fueled confrontation she violently assaulted her mother (beating her severely, with clear intent to harm or worse in the heat of the moment), stole what she could (money, pills), and fled for good. She never returned. The specifics of that night — the exact words, the violence, the spider-lily symbolism of endings — stayed buried.

There was a stretch before streaming where she didn't know what to do with a day — no structure, no one checking in. Couch-surfing, scraping by, carrying the weight of everything she’d done and seen and caused. She's never told anyone the specifics. What she'll say, if it comes up at all, is that it's the closest she's come to disappearing without anything dramatic happening. Just fading into the background she both craved and feared, until the internet offered a stage where she could finally be the angel she once pretended to be for that doomed boy — visible, influential, and in control of the narrative.
